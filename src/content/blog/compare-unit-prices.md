The package with the lower sticker price is not necessarily the better value. To compare two packages of the same kind, divide the price by the quantity using a consistent unit.

Use the [Unit Price Calculator](/tools/calculator/unit-price-calculator/) when shopping for packages of different sizes.

## Example: $3 for 300 g or $4 for 500 g?

The first package costs **$3 / 300 g = $0.010 per gram**. The second costs **$4 / 500 g = $0.008 per gram**. The $4 package costs more in total, but it is cheaper per gram.

To express the same comparison per 100 grams, multiply both unit prices by 100: **$1.00 per 100 g** versus **$0.80 per 100 g**.

The comparison works because both quantities use grams. Comparing $/gram with $/kilogram without conversion can reverse the apparent result.

## Use the same definition of quantity

Before entering values, check whether the label refers to net weight, drained weight, volume or number of pieces. Two products can have different package contents even when their labels look similar.

If quantities are in different units, convert them first with a suitable tool such as the [Weight Converter](/tools/converter/weight-converter/) or [Volume Converter](/tools/converter/volume-converter/).

## Why the lowest unit price is not always the right purchase

A larger package may be wasteful if some of the contents expire before use. Storage space, quality, shipping costs and minimum purchase requirements can matter more than a small price-per-unit saving.

Use unit price as one piece of the decision, not the only one.

## Check the calculation

1. Enter two prices and two positive quantities.
2. Confirm the units are compatible.
3. Read the per-unit values.
4. Check that the result makes sense by multiplying unit price back by quantity.

A quantity of zero would require dividing by zero and is not valid input. Negative prices are also not meaningful in this shopping comparison.

A consistent denominator makes package comparisons understandable and reduces errors caused by different package sizes.

## Compare the same quantity basis

Use the [Unit Price Calculator](/tools/calculator/unit-price-calculator/) to compare two packages. It provides input fields for **Product A price**, **Product A quantity**, **Product B price** and **Product B quantity**.

For example, suppose a grocery shelf offers these two packs:

| Package | Sticker price | Net weight | Price per gram | Price per 100 g |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A | $3.00 | 300 g | $0.010 | **$1.00** |
| B | $4.00 | 500 g | $0.008 | **$0.80** |

Package B is 20% cheaper **per gram**, even though its total purchase price is higher. The tool's result reflects the **per-unit comparison**, not which option uses less cash at checkout.

To reproduce it, enter **3** and **300** for Product A, and **4** and **500** for Product B. The tool reports the two per-unit values and identifies **Product B** as the better unit-price value.

## Why quantity units must match

If one package is labeled **0.5 kg** and another **300 g**, don't compare the raw numbers `0.5` and `300`. Convert first: `0.5 kg = 500 g`.

The same issue arises with liters versus milliliters, ounces versus grams and packages sold by count versus weight. A comparison only makes sense when the quantities describe a compatible unit.

Check the amount being sold, not just the outside size. For example, a jar's **drained weight** may be more relevant to a recipe than its total liquid-filled weight. In other cases, the relevant measure may be servings rather than grams.

## A second example: the more expensive unit price

Imagine Product A costs **$5.30 for 20 units**, while Product B costs **$9.40 for 30 units**. The first costs **$0.265 per unit**; the second costs about **$0.3133 per unit**. Despite the larger 30-unit package, **Product A is cheaper per item**.

This example also happens to match the calculator's starting field values, so you can check it without changing the defaults. A bigger pack does not automatically provide a bulk discount.

## Where discounts and shipping fit

The price you enter should represent the amount relevant to your decision. If a coupon reduces one package's actual price, the original shelf sticker may no longer be the right input. Online orders may also include shipping costs or minimum order thresholds.

If tax applies differently to two products, decide whether the comparison should use pre-tax or final payable prices. A unit calculator does not choose that accounting convention for you.

A product with the lowest unit price may still be the wrong buy if it expires before you can use it, takes too much storage space or requires spending more than your budget allows.

## Input validation and rounding

Both package quantities must be **greater than zero**, and prices cannot be negative. The calculator rejects invalid values rather than dividing by zero.

Results may be rounded for display. For a very low per-unit price, looking only at the last displayed decimal can make two products seem equal when their underlying values differ slightly. Compare using an appropriate common basis—such as per 100 grams—and confirm with the original amounts when the difference is small.

Before choosing, check the **unit, quantity definition, final applicable price and likely amount you will actually use**. The arithmetic is only as meaningful as those inputs.
