Body mass index, or **BMI**, is a number calculated from weight and height. It can help describe weight relative to height in population health and some screening contexts, but it cannot directly tell you body-fat percentage, fitness or whether a particular person is healthy.

The [Toolsiva BMI Calculator](/tools/calculator/bmi-calculator/) takes **weight in kilograms** and **height in centimeters**. Its result is a numeric BMI rounded to one decimal place, with a broad adult category label.

## The metric BMI formula

BMI is calculated as:

`BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in metres)²`

If your height is in centimeters, divide by 100 first. A height of 170 cm is **1.70 m**, not 170 m.

For a person weighing 70 kg and standing 170 cm tall:

`BMI = 70 ÷ (1.70 × 1.70)`

`BMI = 70 ÷ 2.89 ≈ 24.22`

The calculator displays approximately **24.2**.

| Input | Example |
| --- | ---: |
| Weight | 70 kg |
| Height | 170 cm |
| Converted height | 1.70 m |
| Calculated BMI | **24.2** |

## How to check it in Toolsiva

1. Open the [BMI Calculator](/tools/calculator/bmi-calculator/).
2. Enter `70` under **Weight (kg)**.
3. Enter `170` under **Height (cm)**.
4. Check that the main result is about **24.2**.
5. Read the category as an **adult screening label**, not as medical advice tailored to you.

Both fields must contain numbers greater than zero. A value of `0` or a negative measurement is invalid; the calculator does not invent a meaningful BMI for it.

## How adult BMI categories are commonly described

For adults, a commonly used classification uses these broad ranges:

| BMI | Common adult label |
| --- | --- |
| Under 18.5 | Underweight |
| 18.5 to under 25 | Healthy range |
| 25 to under 30 | Overweight |
| 30 or more | Obesity range |

The Toolsiva tool uses those numerical cutoffs. Category labels are not diagnoses. They do not account for muscle mass, body composition, age-related changes or individual medical circumstances, and they are not appropriate as a stand-alone interpretation for children or teenagers.

For reference, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention explains BMI as a screening measure rather than a diagnosis in its [adult BMI guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/bmi/adult-calculator/index.html).

## Three common mistakes

**Entering the height in metres.** If you type `1.70` into a field expecting centimeters, the tool treats that as 1.70 cm. The resulting value is not comparable to the intended calculation.

**Using pounds without conversion.** Toolsiva's fields currently require kilograms and centimeters. If you have imperial measurements, convert them to the stated units before using the tool.

**Interpreting 24.9 versus 25.0 too literally.** BMI is a continuous number derived from rounded measurements. A tiny difference near a threshold does not determine overall health.

## When BMI is useful—and when it isn't

BMI can provide a consistent number for discussing weight relative to height, tracking a rough trend over time or preparing questions for a health professional. It is **not** a direct measure of body-fat distribution, cardiovascular risk or nutritional status.

For any concern involving weight, growth, pregnancy, medication or a medical condition, consult a qualified clinician rather than relying on a single calculator result. An online utility cannot evaluate a person's history or make a diagnosis.

## Recheck your calculation

If a BMI result looks surprising, verify the units and the conversion to metres. As a quick reasonableness check, a weight of 70 kg and height of 1.70 m leads to a number around 24, not hundreds or thousands.

That unit check is more important than chasing extra decimal places. BMI is useful only when its measurements are entered correctly and the result is interpreted with appropriate limits.
