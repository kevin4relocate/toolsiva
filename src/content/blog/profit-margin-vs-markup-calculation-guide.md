Two businesses can describe the same product as having a **40% margin** or a **66.67% markup**—and both numbers can be correct. The key is which amount sits under the fraction.

Profit margin uses **selling price** as its denominator. Markup uses **cost**. Mixing the two can lead to pricing mistakes, especially if you try to turn a margin target directly into a markup rate.

## Start with profit in dollars

Imagine a product costs **$60** to acquire or make, and you sell it for **$100**.

`Profit = selling price − cost`

`Profit = $100 − $60 = $40`

The $40 is the difference between those two amounts. Here we are treating $60 as the relevant unit cost and $100 as the relevant selling price. Additional business expenses, fees, taxes or returns can change the full financial picture.

## Calculate gross profit margin

For this simplified unit example:

`Margin (%) = (selling price − cost) ÷ selling price × 100`

Insert the numbers:

`$40 ÷ $100 × 100 = 40%`

The margin is **40%**. In other words, 40 cents of each dollar in sales is the difference between this selling price and this chosen cost.

## Calculate markup on the same item

Markup uses cost instead:

`Markup (%) = (selling price − cost) ÷ cost × 100`

`$40 ÷ $60 × 100 ≈ 66.67%`

This is why calling a 40% margin a 40% markup would be inaccurate. The percentages answer different questions.

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Cost | Given | $60 |
| Selling price | Given | $100 |
| Unit profit | $100 − $60 | $40 |
| Profit margin | $40 ÷ $100 | **40%** |
| Markup | $40 ÷ $60 | **66.67%** |

## How to price for a target margin

Suppose you have a cost of $60 and want a **40% margin**. Rearrange the formula:

`Selling price = cost ÷ (1 − target margin as a decimal)`

`$60 ÷ (1 − 0.40) = $100`

Now consider a target **40% markup**, not margin:

`Selling price = cost × (1 + markup rate)`

`$60 × 1.40 = $84`

At $84 selling price, profit is $24 and margin is `$24 ÷ $84 ≈ 28.57%`. A 40% markup does **not** produce a 40% margin.

## Which costs belong in the calculation?

The tool cannot determine what should count as "cost" for your business. Depending on the decision, you might include purchase cost, manufacturing inputs, shipping to your warehouse, packaging, processing fees or other direct costs.

A gross margin on a product is different from **net profit margin for the entire business** after operating expenses, financing costs and taxes. Label calculations carefully so different departments do not compare unlike measures.

## Frequent pricing mistakes

**Treating margin and markup as synonyms.** Check the denominator every time.

**Ignoring refunds and discounts.** If the final selling price falls, the effective margin changes.

**Rounding too early.** Keep enough decimal precision until the final price is set, especially for large quantities.

**Assuming an online calculator makes a pricing recommendation.** It performs arithmetic on your input; it does not know market demand or your cost structure.

The safest workflow is to write down the cost assumptions, calculate the selling price and both percentages, then recheck after discounts and transaction fees.
