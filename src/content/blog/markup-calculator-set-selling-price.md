A markup tells you how much you add **on top of cost** when choosing a selling price. For straightforward cost-plus pricing, it can be a fast way to start. But it is worth distinguishing the amount added, the final price and the profit margin that the price implies.

The [Toolsiva Markup Calculator](/tools/calculator/markup-calculator/) takes two inputs: **Cost** and **Markup percentage**. It returns the selling price, the profit difference and the resulting margin.

## A worked example: $60 cost and 50% markup

Start with a unit cost of **$60**. A 50% markup means adding **50% of $60**, not 50% of whatever the eventual selling price will be.

`Markup amount = $60 × 0.50 = $30`

`Selling price = $60 + $30 = $90`

Now calculate the profit margin on the resulting price:

`Margin = $30 ÷ $90 × 100 ≈ 33.33%`

| Measure | Example value |
| --- | ---: |
| Unit cost | $60 |
| Markup rate | 50% |
| Markup amount / unit profit | $30 |
| Selling price | **$90** |
| Resulting margin | **33.33%** |

Enter `60` and `50` in the [Markup Calculator](/tools/calculator/markup-calculator/) to reproduce these figures.

## The formula for any markup percentage

`Selling price = cost × (1 + markup rate ÷ 100)`

For a **25% markup** on $80:

`$80 × 1.25 = $100`

For a **10% markup** on $80:

`$80 × 1.10 = $88`

The formula works the same way regardless of currency, provided the cost is entered in the currency you intend to price in and the result is interpreted in that same currency.

## Why the resulting margin is not the markup

Markup and margin have different denominators:

- **Markup = profit ÷ cost**
- **Margin = profit ÷ selling price**

Using the $60 → $90 example, profit is $30. Dividing $30 by $60 yields 50% markup; dividing it by $90 yields 33.33% margin.

If your business specifically needs to achieve a **target margin**, do not enter that percentage as markup. Use the [Profit Margin Calculator](/tools/calculator/profit-margin-calculator/) to compare the numbers and solve the corresponding target-margin price formula.

## What if markup is zero or negative?

A zero-percent markup makes the selling price equal the cost. There is no difference between those two amounts, before other expenses.

The Toolsiva tool also allows a **negative markup rate down to −100%**, representing pricing below the entered cost. For example, `−25%` on $60 produces a selling price of $45, a **$15 unit loss** against that cost. This may arise during clearance sales, but the arithmetic does not say whether the strategy is sensible.

A rate below −100% is rejected because it would imply a negative selling price in this model. An input of −100% produces a selling price of zero, for which percentage margin is undefined.

## Account for real selling conditions

A cost-plus formula does not include every condition that affects profitability. Before treating a computed price as ready for the store, consider:

- Marketplace or payment processing fees.
- Shipping, storage and returns.
- Tax rules and whether displayed consumer prices must include tax.
- Bulk discounts and coupon promotions.
- Fixed overhead that is not part of the unit cost.

For example, a product with a $60 cost and a $90 price appears to earn $30 per unit under this simplified calculation. If a platform deducts a $9 fee per sale and that fee was omitted from cost, the actual contribution after that fee is only $21. The calculator is not wrong—the **cost input was incomplete** for that decision.

## Verify prices after discounts

Suppose a $90 item is discounted by 20%:

`$90 × (1 − 0.20) = $72`

With the original $60 cost, the revised unit profit is $12 and margin is `12 ÷ 72 ≈ 16.67%`.

The [Discount Calculator](/tools/calculator/discount-calculator/) can compute the sale price; then use Profit Margin Calculator to check the resulting margin.

Use markup as one transparent piece of your pricing workflow, not as a substitute for knowing your true cost or checking whether the market will accept the price.
